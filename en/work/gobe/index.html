01 · Problem

Beginners can't tell which answer is right

By early October 2026, the Clinical Toxicology Centre in Ljubljana had treated 21 mushroom poisonings that year, and 533 over five years. Toxicologist Dr Lucija Šarc warns that families often check online which mushrooms they ate only after the meal.

Every day, beginners in Facebook foraging groups post a photo of a mushroom they've just picked and ask strangers about it. They often get two opposite answers: one person says it's poisonous, the next says it's edible. A beginner has no way to judge which answer is right.

Why the beginner who wants to learn? The problem is biggest for them, and existing solutions serve them worst: experts advise them against identification apps, and answers in groups contradict each other. The product can give them honest value without promising certainty. I'm one of them, so I can test every step on myself. And it's the path experts support, and their support is what trust depends on.

03 · Key decision

Why I gave up photo identification

The obvious idea: take a photo, and the app tells you what it is. It would be faster and bring more downloads.

  1. Users don't ask for a name.

    In the forest they want to know whether they can eat it. No app can answer that with certainty.

  2. A photo can't see the key features.

    The volva at the base of the stem is often underground; smell and colour change when cut don't show in a picture. These are exactly the features that separate deadly mushrooms from edible ones.

  3. The risk isn't symmetrical.

    A wrong “edible” can kill. A wrong “don't know” costs a full basket.

  4. A quick answer teaches nothing.

    The beginner stays dependent on the app and is back at square one next time.

  5. Experts would reject it.

    Under my post in a foraging group, an experienced forager wrote that beginners should avoid identification apps, because a wrong identification can cause severe poisoning. Every word is true.

What I built instead: a quiz that asks what an experienced forager looks at: where it grows, which trees are nearby, what's under the cap, whether it has a ring and a volva. Step by step it rules out what doesn't fit, and next to every mushroom it shows the dangerous lookalikes.

It doesn't identify mushrooms. It teaches you to look.

The product promise

The cost: fewer downloads and a slower answer. So the success criterion is that a beginner gets from opening the app to possible matches in under 60 seconds.

04 · In the user's shoes

Designed for the forest, not the couch

  • One hand, in a forest

    Large touch targets, main actions in the lower half of the screen, contrast that holds in sunlight. Works offline and asks for neither camera nor location.

  • Colour is a soft signal

    The death cap often has an olive-brown cap, and a beginner says “brown”. So colour never rules a mushroom out; it only moves it down the list.

  • “Don't know” is a valid answer

    It's on every question and never rules out a dangerous mushroom. The “How do I check?” help has photos.

  • A test at home

    My daughter brought home a mushroom: a parasol, or a deadly destroying angel. The app didn't choose for us. It showed us the difference: snake-like scales on the stem.

  • A test in the forest

    One walk showed there are far more common mushrooms than 25. Testers reported a missing mushroom 33 times. Before launch: 64 species.

  • A bug from the meadow

    “In a meadow” plus “Smooth” gave 0 possible mushrooms. The fix was a new screen: “No mushroom in the app matches. Don't eat a mushroom you can't recognise.”

06 · Decisions

Decisions and trade-offs

Every screen starts as a mock-up I approve before any code. I chose between ideas with an impact–effort matrix.

A quiz instead of a camera

Why

Photo identification promises a certainty that doesn't exist.

Trade-off

Fewer downloads, a slower answer.

Deadly species are never silently dropped

Why

The risk isn't symmetrical: a wrong “edible” can kill.

Trade-off

A longer list of possible matches.

Soft and hard signals

Why

Beginners misjudge colour, so colour never rules a mushroom out.

Trade-off

A less narrow shortlist.

Lookalikes even when the app doesn't know them

Why

Silence about a lookalike must not mean “safe”.

Trade-off

I publicly admit what the app doesn't know.

No internet, camera or location

Why

There's no signal in the forest; privacy.

Trade-off

All photos ship inside the app, so the install is bigger.

Sources instead of waiting for expert review

Why

Launch doesn't depend on a volunteer; when sources disagree, the stricter label wins.

Trade-off

Responsibility for the content is mine.

64 species instead of 25 before launch

Why

33 reports of a missing mushroom and one walk in the forest.

Trade-off

A later launch.

Analytics only with consent

Why

Trust and GDPR; no IP addresses, no search terms.

Trade-off

I see about 57% of users.

07 · Measurement

What people actually do

Analytics (PostHog, EU servers) starts only after the user taps “Allow”. No IP addresses, no session recordings, no search terms.

During the test, testers mostly tapped through the quiz on the couch. Data like that measures curiosity, not use. The real test was the forest.

Google's test robots were being counted as users (28 fake devices). After I fixed the filter, there were 57 active devices, not 87.

09 · What I learned

Four lessons for every next product

  • Letting go is hardest

    The hardest product decision is dropping the feature everyone expects.

  • Test where it's used

    Couch data measures curiosity. The real test is the place where the product is used.

  • Measure before the test

    I add analytics before the test. Without it I'd have a feeling, not evidence.

  • Check the filter

    If the number is the same with and without it, the filter doesn't work.
