01 · Why I started

I wanted to do the whole thing myself, once

From idea to launch, with analytics from day one: what I measure, how I build the funnel, where privacy draws the line.

I had several ideas: an app, a journal, a website. I chose with an impact–effort matrix. Mushrooms won.

I don't know mushrooms. That turned out to be an advantage: I could test every step on myself.

02 · Insight

People aren't looking for knowledge. They're looking for an answer.

Every day, beginners in Facebook foraging groups post a photo of a mushroom and ask strangers. Under the same photo, one person writes “poisonous”, the next “edible”. A beginner can't tell who is right.

By October 2026, the Clinical Toxicology Centre in Ljubljana had treated 21 mushroom poisonings that year, and 533 over five years. Toxicologist Dr Lucija Šarc warns that families often check what they ate only after the meal.

One more answer doesn't solve this. An app that teaches people to judge does.

It doesn't identify mushrooms. It teaches you to look at them.

04 · How I built it with AI

With AI, building anything is fast. Building the right thing is harder.

I'm not a developer. Claude Code wrote the code; the decisions were mine.

An app in a day didn't interest me. With AI you quickly get something that works but has no head or tail. I looked for a balance between three things.

  1. Instructions

    On day one, a product document: purpose, non-goals, safety rules, success criteria. In the AI's instructions, rules that apply everywhere: never invent a source; anything without one is marked “source missing”.

  2. Management

    A plan in nine phases, with a reason for the order. Design before screens, so I build them once. Data before screens, so I test on real mushrooms. Analytics once the app is whole.

  3. Control

    Every screen starts as a mock-up. Code comes only after I approve it. A review and tests after every phase: 542 by the end.

In the end the product reflected the original goal, not whatever was easiest to build.

05 · What I had to watch out for

A wrong “edible” can kill

  1. The quiz never silently rules out a deadly mushroom.

    The risk isn't symmetrical. A wrong “don't know” costs a full basket; a wrong “edible” can cost a life.

  2. “Don't know” is always allowed.

    It never rules out a dangerous mushroom.

  3. Colour is a soft sign.

    The death cap often has an olive-brown cap, and a beginner says “brown”. So colour doesn't rule a mushroom out, it only moves it down.

  4. Every fact has a source.

    When sources disagree, the stricter label wins.

  5. Law and trust.

    Protected species by regulation, picking rules by the Mycological Association's code, analytics only after “Allow”.

06 · Shortcuts I didn't take

Where I could have gone faster, and didn't

  1. Photo identification

    A quiz that teaches you to look. A photo can't see a volva in the soil, a smell, or a colour change when cut. The price: fewer downloads.

  2. A demo with 8 mushrooms for LinkedIn

    A full Google Play launch. Sources, terms of use, a privacy policy and a closed test. I decided on day two.

  3. Launching with 25 species

    64 species. Testers reported 33 times that their mushroom was missing. I checked every new species against sources.

  4. Data from the sofa

    A test in the forest. Testers mostly clicked through the quiz at home. That kind of data measures curiosity, not use.

  5. Raw numbers

    A filter for test devices. Google's test bots counted as users. Active devices weren't 87, but 57.

07 · Where I kept it simple

What I didn't build

  • Accounts and a server

    Nobody signs in. Everything is inside the app.

  • Internet, camera and location

    There's no signal in the forest. Fewer permissions, more privacy.

  • Waiting for an expert review

    Google Play doesn't require one. The requirement was in my own spec; I replaced it with sources.

  • An app for every forager

    One main user, one promise.

  • Warnings on every update

    The warning shows on first launch. Too many warnings and people stop reading.

  • Ads

    An ad next to a warning about a deadly mushroom would erode trust.

08 · What the data showed

People open the dangerous lookalikes

The most opened mushroom in the first week was the false chanterelle, a poisonous lookalike of the chanterelle. Second was the deadly dapperling, a deadly lookalike of the parasol. The porcini isn't in the top eight.

55% of opens happen at the weekend, when people go foraging. The numbers are small, 46 people with analytics in one week, but the direction is clear.

They're not just looking for a name. They're looking for the difference between edible and poisonous.

09 · What I learned

For every next product

  • Letting go is hardest

    The hardest decision is dropping the feature everyone expects.

  • Building with AI is fast

    The first working version was ready in a week. What improved the app was the slow part: testing, sources and reviews.

  • Test where it's used

    The real test is the place where the product is used.

  • Measure before testing

    Without analytics I'd have had a feeling, not evidence. I test the filter: if a number is the same with and without it, it doesn't work.
